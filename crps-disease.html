What CRPS is

CRPS is a chronic regional pain condition, almost always in a hand, arm, foot, or leg, that is more severe or longer-lasting than expected after an injury, fracture, surgery, or immobilisation. Pain is regional (not one dermatome or one cut nerve). Colour, temperature, sweating, swelling, movement, hair, or nails are usually abnormal on the same limb.

WHO ICD-11 classifies it as chronic primary pain (MG30.04), with a secondary link to post-traumatic / post-surgical pain. In German ICD-10-GM practice it is often coded G90.5 (Typ I / Morbus Sudeck) or G90.6 (Typ II / Kausalgie).

It is not “all in the mind,” though anxiety, sleep loss, and fear of moving the limb commonly follow and can worsen disability. A specific personality type does not cause CRPS.

Types and names

Type I no major nerve cut

Former name: reflex sympathetic dystrophy (RSD), Sudeck, shoulder-hand syndrome. Most cases.

Type II known nerve injury

Former name: causalgia. Same clinical picture as type I, plus a documented peripheral nerve lesion. Signs spread beyond that nerve’s territory — unlike ordinary neuropathic pain.

Partial remission Valencia 2021

Previously met full Budapest criteria; now fewer signs, but leftover pain/sensitivity can still disable work and sleep.

CRPS-NOS

Some CRPS-like features, never fully Budapest-positive, and no better alternative diagnosis.

Warm vs cold (informal)

Warm: hot, red, swollen — often early/inflammatory. Cold: cool, pale/blue — more often longer-standing. Not official IASP subtypes yet.

Old “3 stages”

Bonica’s acute → dystrophic → atrophic timeline is not reliable. Duration does not map cleanly onto stages.

Risk factors with relatively consistent signals

  • Highest-yield: severe pain in week 1 after trauma (wrist-fracture NRS 7–8/10 predicted CRPS at 4 months).
  • Female sex; age 50–80; upper limb in many Western series.
  • Immobilisation (tight cast / unused limb).
  • Open fracture or crush vs simple closed fracture.
  • Asthma (OR ~3.0), ACE inhibitors (OR ~2.7), diabetes (OR ~3.2), migraine, RA, widespread pain.
  • Post-injury anxiety / catastrophising associated with onset or persistence — not proof of a psychogenic cause.

Clinical features

Onset is usually days to a few weeks after trauma (often within 8 weeks). Typically one distal limb. Bilateral-from-the-start pain argues against CRPS.

CategoryWhat patients notice / what examiners find
SensoryBurning, stabbing, or electric pain; allodynia; hyperalgesia
VasomotorRed / mottled / pale / blue; hot or cold vs the other side
Sudomotor / edemaPuffy limb; extra sweat or very dry skin
Motor / trophicWon’t move, weak, tremor, dystonia; shiny skin, hair/nail change; later contracture or bone thinning

Burden beyond the limb: poor sleep, fatigue, depression/anxiety, a limb that “doesn’t feel like mine,” and job loss. Up to ~40% do not return to work; about one-third of those who do need adapted duties.

Mechanisms (multifactorial)

Inflammation

TNF-α, IL-6 and neuropeptides (substance P, CGRP, bradykinin) → redness, heat, swelling, sensitised nociceptors.

Immune / autoantibody

Antibodies to adrenergic and muscarinic receptors reported; Ig transfer can mimic CRPS in animals.

Peripheral + central sensitisation

Lowered pain thresholds, spinal wind-up, NMDA involvement; allodynia far beyond the scar.

Brain map + autonomic

Shrinkage of the cortical hand/foot map; colour/temp/sweat asymmetry; sympathetic–nociceptor coupling in some people.

Genetics: possible HLA/TNF links; 2023 exome hints (ANO10, P2RX7, PRKAG1, SLC12A9) in a subset. Not a single-gene disease.

Treatment — four equal pillars (UK RCP)

High-certainty CRPS-specific drug trials are scarce. Care is early, multidisciplinary, and function-first. Cochrane overviews rate most single interventions as low or very-low certainty.

1. Education

  • Pain is nerve- and inflammation-driven, not invented
  • Many improve in the first 18 months
  • Pacing, flare plans, family involved

2. Rehab (PT/OT)

  • Desensitisation, gentle ROM, graded load
  • Mirror therapy / graded motor imagery
  • Avoid both forced pain and total disuse

3. Pain relief

  • Simple analgesics first
  • Then neuropathic agents (TCA, SNRI, gabapentinoid) as for neuropathic pain
  • Early bisphosphonate sometimes; short steroids mixed evidence
  • RCP: do not start strong opioids for CRPS

4. Psychology

  • CBT / pain programmes
  • Sleep, fear of movement, mood
  • Not “personality treatment”

Procedures

  • Sympathetic blocks: historically used; RCTs suggest they are probably ineffective for pain.
  • IV guanethidine: not recommended.
  • Spinal cord or dorsal-root-ganglion stimulation: specialist option after failed integrated care (ACCURATE favoured DRG vs SCS in selected CRPS). Infection, lead failure, and fading benefit occur.
  • Amputation is not a reliable cure (phantom/stump pain, recurrent CRPS).
  • Ketamine infusions: possible short-lived relief; not first-line; psychomimetic risk.

Prevention (uncertain)

Comfortable (not overly tight) casts and early movement after fracture. Vitamin C 500 mg/day for 50 days after wrist fracture: mixed meta-analyses — not a firm mandate.

Prognosis numbers

  • Rapid improvement is common in the first 6 months.
  • At 12 months, about 14–27% still meet Budapest criteria in prospective series. One CRPS I cohort still had 48% Budapest-positive a year later.
  • NEJM 2025: roughly 80% substantial improvement by 18 months; later big gains are less common.
  • From year 1 to year 8, average severity may barely change. People can feel much better and still tick residual criteria (mild pain, sensitivity, occasional swell).
  • No improvement by ~18 months: higher risk of spread or non-CRPS widespread pain.
  • Work: up to 40% do not return; ~33% of returners need adapted work.

Early recognition and rehab are repeatedly linked to better function, even though many drug trials are disappointing.

Important mimics (diagnosis of exclusion)

Budapest item 4 fails if another disease explains the limb. Common alternatives:

  • Cellulitis / infection
  • Deep-vein thrombosis
  • Arterial insufficiency / PAD
  • Raynaud, erythromelalgia, vasculitis
  • Lymphedema
  • Small- or large-fibre neuropathy; ordinary post-op neuropathic pain
  • Inflammatory arthritis, gout
  • Acute compartment syndrome

Tests (thermography, 3-phase bone scan, QSART, duplex ultrasound, labs) help rule out mimics. They do not prove CRPS by themselves.
